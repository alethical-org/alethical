Follow the Money: Why Outsiders in Both Parties Are Fighting the Same Fight

Not what candidates say — who writes the checks. Minnesota’s money doesn’t divide into Republicans and Democrats. It divides into insiders and outsiders, and that line cuts straight through both parties.

THE SHORT VERSION

The outsider candidates in the Republican party and the outsider candidates in the DFL are funded almost identically: small donations from regular people, and their local county party. The insiders in both parties are also funded almost identically: PACs, lobbyists and party machinery — often the same PACs and the same lobbyists.

The two party establishments have more in common with each other than either has with its own grassroots. That is not an opinion. It is arithmetic, and every number below can be checked.

Where the money actually goes

Most political money in Minnesota never touches a candidate. Over the last eleven years, all 1,700-odd candidates for state office raised about $108 million between them. The four legislative caucus committees and the two state parties took in about $221 million $223 million CORRECTED SEP 2 2026$221 million — twice as much. Another $179 million was spent independently, on advertising and mail about candidates, money those candidates never see and cannot direct. More than half of it paid to attack someone rather than to support anyone.

So the candidate on your ballot is usually the smallest financial player in their own race. The real money sits one level up, in the party machinery — and whoever funds the machinery has more influence over it than any donor to any candidate.

The same donors fund both parties

Filter the records to donors who gave to both a Republican and a DFL committee in the same two-year cycle and a short, familiar list appears: trade associations, utilities, health systems, gambling interests, the building trades, and the lobbying firms that carry them. They do not split evenly, and they do not need to. They fund whoever holds the gavel.

Establishment candidates in the two parties therefore share more of their donor list with each other than with the outsiders wearing their own party label.

The outsiders look the same — in both parties

Outsider candidates in the two parties are roughly four times more similar to each other, in where their money comes from, than to their own party’s establishment. The two establishments are the most similar pair of all.

The clearest single measure is donor breadth. An establishment candidate’s average dollar arrives from someone who also funds 80 to 150 other committees. An outsider’s average dollar comes from someone who funds about a dozen.

HOW WE SCORED THIS

Outsider and establishment are ours — nobody filed those words. We scored every candidate committee that raised $20,000 or more since 2021, on records through Aug 11 2026, on four things: the share of its money from PACs, the share arriving in gifts of $200 or less, support from local party units, and how many other committees each of its donors funds. Nothing in the score reads a candidate’s politics, their votes, or what they say.

Small money against big money

Minnesota names a donor only once their giving passes $200 in total for the year, so the smallest gifts are counted but never itemised. That unnamed share is itself a signal: on outsider committees it is routinely the largest single line in the filing, while establishment committees raise most of what they raise in named four-figure gifts and PAC transfers.

Two campaigns can report the same total and have been funded by populations that barely overlap — a few hundred donors against a few dozen.

The Senate race

The pattern holds in this year’s Senate primary, where the same split is visible in federal filings: the establishment candidates’ money arrives through PACs and out-of-state bundlers, the outsiders’ through small in-state gifts and local party units.

We hold no federal records, so nothing here links into this site. Those figures come from Schedule A of each committee’s FEC filings, with duplicate earmark rows removed, and are public at the FEC.

Why the outsiders can’t see each other

Because they never share a ballot. Each fights their own party’s establishment in a primary the other party’s outsiders never see, against an opponent funded from the same handful of sources. The fight looks partisan from inside it and looks identical from outside it.

What it means for a movement

If the dividing line in the money is inside each party rather than between them, then the durable question for a voter is not which party a candidate belongs to but who funded the campaign — and that is answerable, per candidate, from public records, in about five minutes.

The one rule we follow

We never claim to know why anyone gave money. That a PAC funds both parties is a fact. What it wants in return is a question, and the filings do not answer it. Everything above comes from records anyone can open — the point is to make opening them take five minutes instead of five months.
